Operation Paperclip: America's Secret Recruitment of Nazi Scientists

TL;DR

Between 1945 and 1959, the U.S. brought more than 1,600 German scientists, engineers and technicians stateside under Operation Paperclip, run by the Joint Intelligence Objectives Agency. The most famous recruit, V-2 engineer Wernher von Braun — a Nazi Party and SS member — became chief architect of the Saturn V. Dossiers were systematically whitewashed to get recruits past Truman's own 1946 exclusion order.

Operation Paperclip was the U.S. program that relocated German scientific talent after WWII. The Joint Intelligence Objectives Agency (JIOA), established in 1945 as a subcommittee of the Joint Intelligence Committee under the Joint Chiefs of Staff, ran the program; field work was largely carried out by U.S. Army Counterintelligence Corps agents in coordination with the OSS, the CIA's precursor. The target list: aerospace, rocketry, chemical warfare and other fields of strategic interest as the Cold War took shape.

The prize recruit was Wernher von Braun, technical director at Peenemünde and lead engineer of the V-2 rocket. A documented member of the Nazi Party who rose to SS-Sturmbannführer, von Braun later became director of NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center and chief architect of the Saturn V rocket that carried the Apollo missions.

The whitewashing is a matter of record. President Truman's directive of September 3, 1946 authorized bringing 1,000 scientists but explicitly excluded anyone found 'to have been a member of the Nazi Party, and more than a nominal participant in its activities, or an active supporter of Nazi militarism.' Investigative journalist Linda Hunt's 1985 exposé in the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists showed the exclusion was routinely circumvented: she examined 130+ Paperclip dossiers and found every one altered to remove security-threat classifications — von Braun's own September 1947 report calling him 'a potential security threat' was rewritten by February 1948 to read 'no derogatory information.'

The full paper trail kept surfacing for decades — through Hunt's book Secret Agenda (1991), Christopher Simpson's Blowback (1988), and the document releases forced by the Nazi War Crimes Disclosure Act of 1998. The program delivered enormous technological gains and an enduring ethical stain: national security used as a reason to launder the records of men tied to a criminal regime.

Evidence status: Confirmed by primary sources — JIOA records, Truman's September 3, 1946 directive, and NARA's Nazi War Crimes Disclosure Act releases.
